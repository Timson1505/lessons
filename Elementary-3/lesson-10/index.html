ARPANET: A secret project

On April 30, 1995, a quiet technical ceremony took place in a government office in Washington, D.C. No cameras flashed. No crowds cheered. But on that day, the internet officially went public. What had been a secret military tool for decades suddenly became available to every ordinary citizen. Few people understood what had just happened. Even fewer realized that a technology the government had kept hidden for nearly 40 years was now free. The door to a new world had opened—and nobody could close it again.

Before the internet became the internet, it had been a military project called ARPANET. In the late 1950s, the United States Department of Defense had faced a terrifying problem. They asked a simple question: What happens if the Soviet Union bombs our communication centers? The traditional telephone system had relied on central hubs. Destroy the hub, and the entire network would collapse.

The military engineers had proposed a revolutionary idea. Instead of one central point, they had imagined a distributed network—a web of connections where every computer could talk to every other computer. If one node went down, the information had already found another path. By 1969, they had successfully connected four university computers.

If the technology existed in the 1950s and 1960s, why had governments kept it a secret from the public for so long? The answer is both practical and political. First, the military had viewed the network as a strategic weapon. They had not wanted their enemies to know about it. Second, and more critically, they had understood something important: a connected public is a powerful public. Information is power, and governments have always controlled the flow of information. Before the internet, television, radio, and newspapers had already been centralized—easy to monitor and manage. A decentralized network, however, had presented a new kind of threat. How do you control a crowd that can talk to itself without permission?

How Governments Built the Social Media Trap Without Getting Their Hands Dirty

In the late 1990s, a strange and wonderful dream had taken hold of the public imagination. The internet was going to be a digital utopia: free, borderless, and democratic. Facebook, Instagram, and WhatsApp had not yet been invented, but the stage had already been set by a curious partnership—one that nobody talked about openly. What if the social media world we now live in had never been a pure gift of Silicon Valley genius? What if it had been, from the start, the most elegant government mind-control project ever designed, executed in such a way that the government itself could wash its hands of any responsibility?

The secret of avoiding responsibility is simple: never do the dirty work yourself. After World War II, intelligence agencies like the CIA and the Pentagon’s advanced research arm, DARPA, had already learned a hard lesson. The public had begun to distrust large government projects.

This is exactly what happened. DARPA had financed the original protocols that became the internet. The early computer networks that would one day support Facebook had been born inside military research labs. By the late 1990s, the U.S. government had already achieved something brilliant: it had created the technological foundation, then stepped back and encouraged young, idealistic college students to colonize it. The message was clear: "Go ahead, build your platforms, connect the world.

Data collection is only half the story. The true purpose, according to this theory, is control of the mind. A population that is anxious, divided, and addicted is easier to control. The algorithm that keeps you scrolling had not been designed by a government psychologist—but it was funded by an ecosystem that serves government interests. The beauty of the scheme is this: no single person is ever fully responsible. The government points to the company, the company points to the user agreement, and the user stares at the glowing screen.

The question now is: Was Snowden a hero or a traitor? And more importantly, now that we know what we know, what do we do with the phone in our hand?
