A map of culture

Before I traveled to Europe, I always imagined European countries and people are similar, everyone can speak English. When you enter to the EU, all borders between countries disappear. But, do culture and language borders disappear? Everybody knows where Europe starts, but nobody knows where it ends. Civilization - is not only military and economic power, but also good life conditions of citizens and culture. So, are Russia and Turkie European countries? Let's find out.

In 1575, a French ambassador, traveling to Moscow, crossed into what is now Western Poland. He has written in his diary: "We have left the civilized world behind. The roads have turned to mud, the villages have become poor, and the people have looked at us with a mixture of fear and suspicion." He has not seen a comfortable hotel for weeks. He has noticed that the Latin language—the international language of educated men—has completely disappeared. The architecture has changed from Renaissance stone to simple wood. In his mind, he has crossed a border of civilization. To him, the East has begun.

Now, jump to 1965. A British university student has decided to travel from London to Moscow by train. He has heard about the "Iron Curtain". But he has not expected it to be so real.

After passing through Vienna, he has arrived at the Hungarian border. He has immediately felt the change. "The color of the world has changed," he writes. "Western Europe has been colorful, full of advertisements and noisy traffic. But here, the buildings are gray. The people are quiet and do not smile freely. We have entered a different atmosphere."

So, was Europe always divided by a deep, ancient line between "civilized" and "backward"? Or have politicians in the 20th century artificially created a division where none naturally existed? The traveler from the 16th century has shown us that the idea of a "backward East" is old. But the student from the 20th century has shown us how the Iron Curtain has hardened.

Homework

Lego

For over 90 years, the LEGO Group has separated itself as more than just a toy company. It has literally crossed every cultural border, from its home in Denmark to playrooms across the globe. In a world of fleeting digital trends, the simple plastic brick has never lost its influence. The custom of clicking two bricks together is a universal language. While many competitors have produced similar bricks, the perception of LEGO's "clutch power" and quality has remained unmatched.

The company's journey has not been easy. In the early 2000s, LEGO nearly went bankrupt after chasing trends that separated it from its core identity. This near-collapse has since become a famous business case study. The management has admitted their perception was wrong; they had confused innovation with abandoning tradition. By returning to their core custom of inspiring creative play, LEGO achieved a remarkable turnaround. Today, the brand has built a legacy that gives children a unique kind of freedom to imagine and construct. It operates almost like a friendly embassy, spreading a message of structured creativity from its headquarters to every nation, without the need for any embassy buildings to sign a treaty.
