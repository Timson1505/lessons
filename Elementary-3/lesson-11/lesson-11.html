Grammar: Mixed conditional

Mixed conditionals connect different times – usually a past action with a present result, or a present state with a past result.

Rule If + past perfect, + would
A past action directly affects the present situation If I had studied medicine, I would be a doctor now.
Rule If + simple past, + would have + past participle
A present fact explains why something happened in the past. If I were richer, I would have bought that car yesterday.

A broken dream

Daniel and his younger brother Leo grew up in a poor neighborhood where survival was more important than dreams. Their mother worked at a laundry shop, and their father had died when the boys were still children. Despite their difficult life, Daniel had extraordinary talent in boxing. Coaches believed he could become a national champion.

Leo was intelligent and ambitious. He dreamed of studying medicine at university, but the family could not afford tuition fees. Daniel decided to support him. He worked nights at a warehouse while continuing his boxing training. However, the money was never enough.

One evening, Daniel’s friend offered him “easy money” for helping with a robbery. Daniel refused at first, but after their mother became seriously ill, he became desperate. “If I weren’t responsible for this family, I wouldn’t even think about committing a crime,” Daniel told himself. The robbery went wrong. A security guard was injured, and Daniel was arrested. He was sentenced to eight years in prison. His boxing career ended immediately. While Daniel was in prison, Leo finished medical school and eventually became a successful doctor. Yet he never visited his brother often because he felt ashamed and angry.

Years later, Daniel was released. He found that life outside prison was harder than he had imagined. Nobody wanted to hire an ex-prisoner, and he earned only a living wage working at a car wash.

One rainy evening, the brothers finally argued about the past. Daniel shouted:
“If I hadn’t sacrificed my future for you, I wouldn’t be living like this now!” “If I hadn’t committed that offence, I could have become a champion.”

Leo answered quietly:
“If you were more patient back then, you wouldn’t have gone to prison.” “But if you hadn’t helped me, I wouldn’t be a doctor today.”

Daniel looked away and said:
“If I were successful now, maybe people would respect me again.” “If our family hadn’t been so poor, none of this would have happened.”

For the first time, Leo understood the pain his brother had carried for years. He realized Daniel had destroyed his own future to protect the family. At the end of the story, Leo offered Daniel a job at a sports rehabilitation center. Daniel accepted, although he still regretted his past. As he watched teenagers train every day, he often thought: "If I hadn't made one terrible decision, my life would be completely different now."
But sometimes, when a young boxer thanked him for a piece of advice, he felt something close to peace.
If I am no longer a fighter, he told himself, at least I can teach others how to fight.

Exercise 5: Discuss these questions below.

  1. Was Daniel responsible for his crime, or was poverty partly responsible?
  2. If you were Daniel, would you sacrifice your future?
  3. Do older siblings sometimes sacrifice too much for their families?
  4. Did Leo behave unfairly toward Daniel?
  5. Is earning only a living wage enough for a person to live with dignity?
  6. If their mother hadn’t become ill, would Daniel have committed the offence?

The Last Sacrifice

Elena Martinez lived in a small apartment with her younger brother, Carlos. The roof leaked when it rained. Their mother had died. Their father had left years ago. Carlos was seventeen. He was very sick. His kidneys were failing. Without a private surgery, he would die within a year. The surgery cost $15,000. Elena cleaned houses for a living wage. It was not enough. If I were richer, she thought, I wouldn't be so worried every day.

One night, Elena walked past a jewelry store. She cleaned there twice a week. She knew the owner kept cash in a drawer. She was desperate. Carlos could not wait anymore. If I weren't so desperate, she told herself, I wouldn't even think about robbing the store. But she thought about it. And then she did it. She wore a mask. She entered through the back door. She took (robbed) $15,000. Exactly what she needed. But a guard was in the bathroom. He came out. He saw her. He shouted. Elena panicked. She pushed him. He fell and hit his head. He did not move.

The guard survived. But Elena had committed a crime. She was sentenced to six years in prison. Carlos was alone. He could not afford the surgery. He became sicker. Elena wrote him letters from prison. He refused to answer. He wrote one letter back: “You became a criminal. I don't want to see you.” Elena shouted when she read it. Then she cried. If I hadn't been so desperate, she whispered, Carlos wouldn't hate me now.

After four years, Elena was released early for good behavior. She was thirty-two. Her hands shook. Her hair had gray streaks. She found Carlos living in a shelter. He was on dialysis. His face was yellow. He looked fifty years old. “Carlos,” she said. “Go away,” he answered. Elena felt it like a stone in her chest. If I had trusted the hospital to help, she thought, he would be dead by now anyway. But at least he wouldn't hate me.

Elena sold her mother's wedding ring. She got $500. She found an illegal kidney donor on the black market. It was dangerous. It was wrong. But she had no choice. She found Carlos on the street. He was vomiting blood. “Take this,” she said, holding a cooler. “It’s a kidney. It’s not legal. But it’s your only chance.” Carlos looked at her. His eyes softened. “Why?” he asked. “Because I sacrificed everything for you. My freedom. My soul. Despite everything, I would do it again.” She paused. “If I hadn’t robbed that store, you would be dead already. Now at least you can live.”
