Housewives in Central Asia

In many Central Asian families, the life of a housewife, or “kelin,” is full of responsibilities, traditions, and hard work. A kelin usually wakes up very early in the morning, sometimes before everybody else in the house. If she lives in a big family, she often prepares breakfast for many people. After that, she cleans the rooms, washes the dishes, makes tea, and gets children ready for school.

In traditional families, a kelin often cooks several times a day. If guests arrive unexpectedly, she prepares more food immediately because hospitality is very important in Central Asian culture. She usually uses the stove, oven, pots, pans, knives, and many kitchen utensils every day. If the family eats together, the kelin serves food first and often eats last herself.

A housewife’s daily routine is not easy. If she does not clean the house regularly, relatives may criticize her. If children become sick, she takes care of them all day and night. Sometimes a kelin feels tired because her work never really ends. While other people finish their jobs and rest, many housewives continue working late into the evening.

In modern Central Asia, opinions about housewives are changing. Some people believe that a woman should focus mainly on family life, while others think women should balance both family and career. If families shared household chores equally, many women would feel less stress and exhaustion.

There are also generational differences. Older relatives sometimes expect young kelins to follow strict traditions. If a young woman refuses certain traditions, conflicts may appear in the family. However, younger generations often believe relationships should be based on partnership and respect.

Personally, I think being a housewife is one of the hardest jobs in society. If people respected domestic work more, housewives would feel more appreciated. At the same time, if families supported women emotionally and financially, many difficulties would become easier to manage.

Homework

Today is my turn

My name is Timson, and I live in an international dormitory with students from many countries. Life in our dorm is never boring because we have a special cooking schedule. Every evening, one student cooks dinner for everybody. We prepare delicious food from all around the world, and the kitchen is always full of noise, laughter, and wonderful smells.

If somebody cooks spicy food, the Indian students become very happy. If students make Italian pasta, everybody asks for more cheese. If people leave dirty pans and forks in the sink, our dorm manager gets angry very quickly.

Our kitchen is not very big, but it has everything we need: a stove, an oven, sharp knives, large pans, pots, plates, spoons, and forks. Some students cook beef, chicken, pork, or fish. Others prepare vegetarian dishes. If the food smells good, students usually come to the kitchen before dinner is ready.

Last Friday, it was finally my turn to cook. I decided to make traditional Kyrgyz dishes: besh-barmak, manty, and plov (ash). I wanted everybody to taste food from my country. If I cook Kyrgyz food, I always use fresh meat, onions, carrots, and many spices.

My friends helped me in the kitchen. Carlos washed the dishes, Ming cut vegetables, and Alex prepared forks and plates for dinner. If students help each other, cooking becomes faster and more enjoyable.

Then I started cooking plov in a deep pan. I fried onions, carrots, garlic, and pieces of beef in hot oil. The smell became incredible. If somebody walks near our kitchen when I cook plov, they always stop and ask, “What is that amazing smell?”

While the food was cooking, my friends asked me many questions about Kyrgyzstan. One student said, “If I visited your country, I would eat besh-barmak every day.” Another friend laughed and said, “If I could cook like you, I would become a famous chef!”

Finally, dinner was ready. I carefully took the hot dishes from the stove and put them on the table. Everybody sat down and started eating like gluttons.

At the end of the evening, there was almost nothing left in the pans. Everybody thanked me and said Kyrgyz food was one of the best dinners in the dormitory. I felt proud and happy. If my mother saw me cooking that night, she would smile proudly too.
